## What this stage does

Streams [TinyStories](https://huggingface.co/datasets/roneneldan/TinyStories) from
the Hub, measures its shape, and writes the sample the tokenizer will be fitted on.

## Why TinyStories

A 15.7M-parameter model trained on general web text produces noise. There simply
isn't capacity for the long tail of English, let alone facts about the world.

TinyStories, from [Eldan & Li (2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07759), exists to
test exactly that boundary. It's synthetic short stories generated by GPT-3.5/4,
constrained to the vocabulary of a 3–4 year old. The distribution is narrow enough
that a very small model can become genuinely *fluent* in it rather than merely
less-bad — the paper's central finding is that models under 10M parameters can
produce coherent, grammatical, plot-consistent English when the domain is
restricted this way.

The tradeoff is the entire reason this project works, and it's worth stating
plainly: **we are buying fluency by giving up generality.** The model will write
good children's stories and be useless at everything else.

## Streaming, and why it's the default at scale

```python
load_dataset("roneneldan/TinyStories", split="train", streaming=True)
```

The full dataset is ~7.6 GB. Downloading it would work on Colab, but the habit is
wrong: at real scale every corpus outgrows local disk, and `streaming=True` is
how you work with data you can only ever see once, in order.

The cost is that you can't shuffle globally or index randomly. That's why stage 2
materializes tokens into a flat file — random access matters during *training*,
just not during ingestion.

## What to look for in the numbers

**Document length distribution.** The number that matters is what fraction of
stories fit inside the 512-token context. Documents longer than the window get
split across training samples, so the model never sees them whole — that bounds
how much narrative structure it can possibly learn. For TinyStories most stories
fit, which is another reason the dataset suits a small-context model.

**Vocabulary coverage.** Very few distinct words carry almost all the text. This
is what justifies the 8192-piece vocabulary in stage 2 — see
[02-tokenizer.md](02-tokenizer.md) for why that number is a parameter-budget
decision rather than a detail.

## Train/validation separation

The validation set is the dataset's own held-out split, never a slice of train.

This matters more than it sounds. If validation data appears in training, the
model memorizes it, perplexity drops, and every downstream decision — when to
stop, whether SFT helped, what quantization costs — is made against a number that
is measuring memorization rather than generalization. Contamination is the single
most common way an evaluation silently becomes meaningless, and the only reliable
defence is to keep the splits separate from the very first stage.

## What we skip, and what it would involve at scale

TinyStories arrives clean. A real pretraining corpus does not, and data work is
usually the largest part of the project:

- **Deduplication** — near-duplicate documents cause memorization and waste
  compute. Usually MinHash/LSH over shingles.
- **Quality filtering** — classifier-based or heuristic scoring to drop
  boilerplate, spam, and machine-generated filler.
- **Decontamination** — removing documents that overlap evaluation benchmarks.
- **PII and safety filtering.**
- **Mixture weighting** — deciding how much code, how much web text, how much
  curated data, and in what order to present it.

None of these change the *shape* of the lifecycle, which is why the project uses a
pre-clean corpus and spends its attention elsewhere.
